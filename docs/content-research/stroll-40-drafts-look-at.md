**How these were written**
- Every fact comes from the place's Wikipedia article, linked under each place. Six facts were
  also checked online on November 14; those stops have a **Checked online** line with sources.
  **⚠ check** marks something to confirm on site before recording.
- Lengths follow the location research guide: prompt up to about 25 words, reveal one sentence,
  story lines up to about 30 words, written for the ear.
- Prompts use the **"Look at…" style** of the 12 places already in the app. Each place also has
  **alt text** for the photo, which describes it for VoiceOver users.
- Some stops have only one or two story lines. That's fine: not every stop needs a long backstory.

## Architecture

### 1. Allen Hall · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** Good link to the Mellon Institute stop.
- **Prompt:** Look at the bronze plaque by the entrance. It honors a famous scientist. Who do you think it is?
- **Reveal:** Marie Curie, who received an honorary degree from Pitt in 1921.
- **Story 1:** The plaque was unveiled in 1969 by a cardinal from Poland, Karol Wojtyła, who nine years later became Pope John Paul the Second.
- **Story 2:** This was the first home of the Mellon Institute, finished in 1914. The institute later moved to the building with the giant columns, just up the street.
- **Story 3:** Its designers decided a science building didn't need women's bathrooms, so most floors still have just one bathroom, alternating men's and women's.
- **Alt text:** A six-story stone building with four tall columns across the middle and a triangular roof above them.
- **Source:** [Allen Hall (University of Pittsburgh)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_Hall_(University_of_Pittsburgh))
- **Note:** Story 2 links to the Mellon Institute columns stop. ⚠ check that the plaque is still by the entrance.

### 2. Carnegie Institute and Library · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** Keep as the one Forbes Avenue stop for the Carnegie building. The four statues make a strong prompt. Needs a street-level photo.
- **Prompt:** Look at the four bronze figures along the Forbes Avenue front, two by the concert hall and two by the museum doors. What do they have in common?
- **Reveal:** Each is a giant of a different field: Shakespeare for writing, Bach for music, Michelangelo for art and Galileo for science.
- **Story 1:** One building holds two museums, a concert hall and the city's main library, all paid for by Andrew Carnegie.
- **Story 2:** Inside, the Hall of Sculpture copies the inside of the Parthenon in white marble.
- **Story 3:** The Hall of Architecture holds the largest collection of plaster casts of famous buildings in America.
- **Alt text:** A long, grand stone building with a row of arched windows and a columned entrance.
- **Source:** [Carnegie Institute and Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnegie_Institute_and_Library)
- **Note:** Same building as stop 4. The photo is an aerial view, so it doesn't show the statues.
- **Checked online (Nov 14):** ✔ Confirmed. The four are John Massey Rhind's "Noble Quartet". Shakespeare and Bach flank the Music Hall steps, and Michelangelo and Galileo sit by the museum entrance, so I fixed the prompt, which had all four at the Music Hall. ([Carnegie Museum of Natural History](https://carnegiemnh.org/facing-outward-looking-ahead-richard-serras-carnegie-as-part-of-an-125-year-legacy-of-architecture-and-outdoor-sculpture/), [City Paper](https://www.pghcitypaper.com/columns/how-were-those-four-statues-outside-the-carnegie-museum-chosen-1337574))

### 5. Panther Hollow Bridge · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** One of the strongest. The panthers are big, obvious and fun to guess.

### 6. Pittsburgh Athletic Association · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** Clear to see from the street, with a good guessing question.

- **Story 1:** Fred Rogers, from Mister Rogers' Neighborhood, swam laps in the pool here every day.

- **Source:** [Pittsburgh Athletic Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pittsburgh_Athletic_Association)
- **Note:** "Palaces along its canals" is a gloss. The source names three specific Venetian buildings.

### 10. Main Building, U.S. Bureau of Mines · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** A good everyday stop for locals: most people walk past Hamburg Hall without knowing it was built to make mining safer.

## History

### 18. Bellefield Boiler Plant · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** The smokestack is easy to see and the answer surprises people.
- **Prompt:** Look at the tall concrete smokestack rising out of the hollow. What do you think this plant heats?
- **Reveal:** Most of Oakland. Its steam warms most of the big buildings around here, including the Cathedral of Learning.
- **Story 1:** Michael Chabon nicknamed it "the Cloud Factory" in his first novel, The Mysteries of Pittsburgh.
- **Story 2:** It was built in 1907 to heat the Carnegie Museum. The concrete stack, from 1966, is 255 feet tall.
- **Story 3:** In its coal days, it could burn a whole 70-ton railroad car of coal in a day. It stopped burning coal in 2009.
- **Alt text:** A brick industrial building in a wooded valley, with a very tall, thin concrete smokestack.
- **Source:** [Bellefield Boiler Plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellefield_Boiler_Plant)

### 19. Log Cabin · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** One of the strongest. The cabin next to the Cathedral is a great contrast, and the salt-store answer is funny.
- **Prompt:** Look at the small log cabin in the shadow of the Cathedral of Learning. What do you think it's used for now?
- **Reveal:** Storing salt for icy winter paths.
- **Story 1:** It stands for how Pitt began in 1787, when classes were said to be held in a log cabin.
- **Story 2:** This cabin dates from the 1820s or 1830s. It was bought at auction for $1,000 and moved here in 1986 for Pitt's 200th birthday.
- **Story 3:** No one knows for sure that Pitt began in a cabin. Fires in 1845 and 1849 destroyed most of its early records.
- **Alt text:** A small log cabin with a stone chimney and a porch, on a lawn below a tall stone tower.
- **Source:** [Log Cabin (University of Pittsburgh)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log_Cabin_(University_of_Pittsburgh))

### 20. Oakland Square Historic District · ✅ Keep

**Verdict: ✅ Keep.** Good, and a pleasant spot to pause.

- **Story 1:** The first 26 houses went up in 1889 and 1890. They were advertised at $6,500 each and sold within months.
- **Story 2:** The developer, Eugene O'Neill, not the famous playwright, grew up in Ireland and probably modeled the square on Dublin's.
- **Story 3:** All 67 houses from the original development are still standing, though many have been changed.
- **Alt text:** A row of tall brick houses with porches and bay windows facing a small tree-filled park.
- **Source:** [Oakland Square Historic District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oakland_Square_Historic_District)
